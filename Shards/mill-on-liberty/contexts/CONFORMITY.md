# CONFORMITY: Is social conformity a threat to individuality comparable to political tyranny?

## Parent context

The parent node establishes that the free development of individuality is essential to well-being. This node identifies what threatens it in Mill's time: not an oppressive state but the pressure of custom and opinion. Mill's core claim is that "society has now fairly got the better of individuality" (Ch. 3, para. 6), and that this social despotism is in one respect worse than political oppression, "penetrating much more deeply into the details of life, and enslaving the soul itself" (Ch. 1, para. 5).

## Key claims from source

- **The diagnosis.** "In our times, from the highest class of society down to the lowest, every one lives as under the eye of a hostile and dreaded censorship. Not only in what concerns others, but in what concerns only themselves, the individual, or the family, do not ask themselves—what do I prefer? or, what would suit my character and disposition? or, what would allow the best and highest in me to have fair-play, and enable it to grow and thrive? They ask themselves, what is suitable to my position? what is usually done by persons of my station and pecuniary circumstances? or (worse still) what is usually done by persons of a station and circumstances superior to mine? I do not mean that they choose what is customary, in preference to what suits their own inclination. It does not occur to them to have any inclination, except for what is customary. Thus the mind itself is bowed to the yoke: even in what people do for pleasure, conformity is the first thing thought of; they live in crowds; they exercise choice only among things commonly done: peculiarity of taste, eccentricity of conduct, are shunned equally with crimes: until by dint of not following their own nature, they have no nature to follow: their human capacities are withered and starved: they become incapable of any strong wishes or native pleasures, and are generally without either opinions or feelings of home growth, or properly their own" (Ch. 3, para. 6).
- **Why social tyranny is more formidable.** Society "practises a social tyranny more formidable than many kinds of political oppression, since, though not usually upheld by such extreme penalties, it leaves fewer means of escape, penetrating much more deeply into the details of life, and enslaving the soul itself" (Ch. 1, para. 5). Its tendency is "to fetter the development, and, if possible, prevent the formation, of any individuality not in harmony with its ways, and compel all characters to fashion themselves upon the model of its own."
- **The despotism of custom.** "The despotism of custom is everywhere the standing hindrance to human advancement, being in unceasing antagonism to that disposition to aim at something better than customary, which is called, according to circumstances, the spirit of liberty, or that of progress or improvement" (Ch. 3, para. 17).

## Arguments and evidence

- **The historical reversal.** "In some early states of society ... the element of spontaneity and individuality was in excess, and the social principle had a hard struggle with it"; law and discipline then "asserted a power over the whole man". "But society has now fairly got the better of individuality; and the danger which threatens human nature is not the excess, but the deficiency, of personal impulses and preferences" (para. 6).
- **The Calvinistic theory as conformity's creed.** "According to that, the one great offence of man is Self-will. All the good of which humanity is capable, is comprised in Obedience. You have no choice; thus you must do, and no otherwise: 'whatever is not a duty, is a sin.' Human nature being radically corrupt, there is no redemption for any one until human nature is killed within him" (para. 7). It "is held, in a mitigated form, by many who do not consider themselves Calvinists". "In some such insidious form there is at present a strong tendency to this narrow theory of life, and to the pinched and hidebound type of human character which it patronises"; many think humans "thus cramped and dwarfed" are "as their Maker designed them to be; just as many have thought that trees are a much finer thing when clipped into pollards" (para. 8).
- **The ideal of having no character.** "The general average of mankind are not only moderate in intellect, but also moderate in inclinations: they have no tastes or wishes strong enough to incline them to do anything unusual, and they consequently do not understand those who have" (para. 15). The reforming public's standard "express or tacit, is to desire nothing strongly. Its ideal of character is to be without any marked character; to maim by compression, like a Chinese lady's foot, every part of human nature which stands out prominently". The result is "weak feelings and weak energies, which therefore can be kept in outward conformity to rule without any strength either of will or of reason" (para. 16). "The greatness of England is now all collective: individually small, we only appear capable of anything great by our habit of combining".
- **Public opinion as the new sovereign.** "At present individuals are lost in the crowd ... The only power deserving the name is that of masses"; "Their thinking is done for them by men much like themselves, addressing them or speaking in their name, on the spur of the moment, through the newspapers" (para. 13). "No government by a democracy or a numerous aristocracy ... ever did or could rise above mediocrity, except in so far as the sovereign Many have let themselves be guided ... by the counsels and influence of a more highly gifted and instructed One or Few."
- **The penalties of nonconformity.** "The man, and still more the woman, who can be accused either of doing 'what nobody does,' or of not doing 'what everybody does,' is the subject of as much depreciatory remark as if he or she had committed some grave moral delinquency." Those who indulge much in doing as they like "are in peril of a commission *de lunatico*, and of having their property taken from them and given to their relations" (para. 14). Diversity of taste is tolerated only where "both those who like each of these things, and those who dislike them, are too numerous to be put down."
- **The forces of assimilation.** Formerly "different ranks, different neighbourhoods, different trades and professions, lived in what might be called different worlds; at present, to a great degree in the same." Political change, education, communications and commerce all promote assimilation, and "a more powerful agency than even all these ... is the complete establishment ... of the ascendency of public opinion in the State", so that "there ceases to be any social support for non-conformity" (para. 18). Europe "is decidedly advancing towards the Chinese ideal of making all people alike", and "unless individuality shall be able successfully to assert itself against this yoke, Europe, notwithstanding its noble antecedents and its professed Christianity, will tend to become another China" (para. 17).
- **Conformity of thought.** The fear of heresy leaves "a large portion of the most active and inquiring intellects" fitting "their own conclusions to premises which they have internally renounced"; the men produced are "either mere conformers to commonplace, or time-servers for truth" (Ch. 2, para. 19). A general State education would likewise be "a mere contrivance for moulding people to be exactly like one another" (Ch. 5, para. 13).

## Distinctions and qualifications

- **Social control was once necessary.** Restraint was justified "since the passions of those who were strong by station or by personal endowment were in a state of habitual rebellion against laws and ordinances, and required to be rigorously chained up" (Ch. 3, para. 6); the objection is to its continuation when the danger has reversed.
- **Society has its own remedy: eccentricity.** "Precisely because the tyranny of opinion is such as to make eccentricity a reproach, it is desirable, in order to break through that tyranny, that people should be eccentric ... That so few now dare to be eccentric, marks the chief danger of the time" (para. 13).
- **Intelligent conformity is not the target.** "An intelligent following of custom, or even occasionally an intelligent deviation from custom, is better than a blind and simply mechanical adhesion to it" (para. 5); customs "have a claim to his deference" as presumptive evidence, but not "merely *as* custom" (para. 3).
- **Resistance must come early.** "It is only in the earlier stages that any stand can be successfully made against the encroachment. The demand that all other people shall resemble ourselves, grows by what it feeds on. If resistance waits till life is reduced *nearly* to one uniform type, all deviations from that type will come to be considered impious, immoral, even monstrous and contrary to nature" (para. 19).
- **Not against combination or progress.** "It is not progress that we object to; on the contrary, we flatter ourselves that we are the most progressive people who ever lived. It is individuality that we war against" (para. 17).
