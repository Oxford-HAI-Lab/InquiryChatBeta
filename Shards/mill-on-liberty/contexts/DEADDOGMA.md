# DEADDOGMA: Do beliefs become empty dogma if never challenged?

## Parent context

The parent node gives four grounds for absolute liberty of discussion. This node is the third and fourth: even a true opinion, "if it is not fully, frequently, and fearlessly discussed, ... will be held as a dead dogma, not a living truth" (Ch. 2, para. 21). Mill's core claim is that the way a truth is held matters as much as its truth: without contest, its grounds are forgotten, then its meaning, and finally its power over conduct.

## Key claims from source

- **Ground 3, as recapitulated.** "Even if the received opinion be not only true, but the whole truth; unless it is suffered to be, and actually is, vigorously and earnestly contested, it will, by most of those who receive it, be held in the manner of a prejudice, with little comprehension or feeling of its rational grounds" (Ch. 2, para. 43).
- **Ground 4.** "The meaning of the doctrine itself will be in danger of being lost, or enfeebled, and deprived of its vital effect on the character and conduct: the dogma becoming a mere formal profession, inefficacious for good, but cumbering the ground, and preventing the growth of any real and heartfelt conviction, from reason or personal experience" (para. 43).
- **Truth held as prejudice is not knowledge.** Where a belief "abides as a prejudice, a belief independent of, and proof against, argument—this is not the way in which truth ought to be held by a rational being. This is not knowing the truth. Truth, thus held, is but one superstition the more, accidentally clinging to the words which enunciate a truth" (para. 22).

## Arguments and evidence

- **Knowing the grounds requires hearing the other side.** "If the cultivation of the understanding consists in one thing more than in another, it is surely in learning the grounds of one's own opinions" (para. 23). Mathematics is the exception, "where there is nothing at all to be said on the wrong side of the question"; but "on every subject on which difference of opinion is possible, the truth depends on a balance to be struck between two sets of conflicting reasons." Cicero "always studied his adversary's case with as great, if not with still greater, intensity than even his own." "He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that ... if he is equally unable to refute the reasons on the opposite side; if he does not so much as know what they are, he has no ground for preferring either opinion." Hearing objections from one's own teachers is not enough: "He must be able to hear them from persons who actually believe them; who defend them in earnest, and do their very utmost for them." "Ninety-nine in a hundred of what are called educated men are in this condition". If opponents "do not exist, it is indispensable to imagine them, and supply them with the strongest arguments which the most skilful devil's advocate can conjure up."
- **Meaning, not only grounds, is lost.** "Not only the grounds of the opinion are forgotten in the absence of discussion, but too often the meaning of the opinion itself. The words which convey it, cease to suggest ideas ... Instead of a vivid conception and a living belief, there remain only a few phrases retained by rote; or, if any part, the shell and husk only of the meaning is retained, the finer essence being lost" (para. 26).
- **The life cycle of creeds.** Doctrines "are all full of meaning and vitality to those who originate them, and to the direct disciples of the originators"; once a creed prevails or stops spreading, "controversy on the subject flags, and gradually dies away", its holders "have generally inherited, not adopted it", and "From this time may usually be dated the decline in the living power of the doctrine" (para. 27). A hereditary creed "remains as it were outside the mind, encrusting and petrifying it against all other influences ... doing nothing for the mind or heart, except standing sentinel over them to keep them vacant."
- **The example of Christianity.** "Not one Christian in a thousand guides or tests his individual conduct by reference to those laws. The standard to which he does refer it, is the custom of his nation, his class, or his religious profession" (para. 28). Christians "believe that the blessed are the poor and humble ... that they should love their neighbour as themselves ... They are not insincere when they say that they believe these things. They do believe them, as people believe what they have always heard lauded and never discussed." "The doctrines have no hold on ordinary believers—are not a power in their minds." The early Christians, of whom enemies said "See how these Christians love one another", "had a much livelier feeling of the meaning of their creed" (para. 29). Today "The sayings of Christ coexist passively in their minds, producing hardly any effect beyond what is caused by mere listening to words so amiable and bland." Doctrines "which are the badge of a sect retain more of their vitality than those common to all recognised sects", because "the peculiar doctrines are more questioned, and have to be oftener defended against open gainsayers. Both teachers and learners go to sleep at their post, as soon as there is no enemy in the field."
- **Proverbs and the "deep slumber".** People "first truly learn the meaning" of familiar maxims "when experience, generally of a painful kind, has made it a reality to them" (para. 30). "The fatal tendency of mankind to leave off thinking about a thing when it is no longer doubtful, is the cause of half their errors. A contemporary author has well spoken of 'the deep slumber of a decided opinion.'"
- **The Socratic remedy.** "The Socratic dialectics, so magnificently exemplified in the dialogues of Plato, were a contrivance of this description. They were essentially a negative discussion of the great questions of philosophy and life, directed with consummate skill to the purpose of convincing any one who had merely adopted the commonplaces of received opinion, that he did not understand the subject—that he as yet attached no definite meaning to the doctrines he professed; in order that, becoming aware of his ignorance, he might be put in the way to attain a stable belief, resting on a clear apprehension both of the meaning of doctrines and of their evidence" (para. 33). Medieval school disputations "had a somewhat similar object", though their premises "were taken from authority, not from reason". "The present modes of education contain nothing which in the smallest degree supplies the place either of the one or of the other." Negative logic, "that which points out weaknesses in theory or errors in practice, without establishing positive truths", is "as a means to attaining any positive knowledge or conviction worthy the name ... cannot be valued too highly".

## Distinctions and qualifications

- **Does truth require permanent disagreement?** Mill states the objection: "Is the absence of unanimity an indispensable condition of true knowledge? Is it necessary that some part of mankind should persist in error, to enable any to realise the truth? ... As soon as mankind have unanimously accepted a truth, does the truth perish within them?" (para. 31). "I affirm no such thing. As mankind improve, the number of doctrines which are no longer disputed or doubted will be constantly on the increase: and the well-being of mankind may almost be measured by the number and gravity of the truths which have reached the point of being uncontested" (para. 32). The loss of live opposition, "though not sufficient to outweigh, is no trifling drawback from, the benefit of its universal recognition", so teachers should "provide a substitute for it; some contrivance for making the difficulties of the question as present to the learner's consciousness, as if they were pressed upon him by a dissentient champion".
- **Dissent should be welcomed even when wrong.** "If there are any persons who contest a received opinion, or who will do so if law or opinion will let them, let us thank them for it, open our minds to listen to them, and rejoice that there is some one to do for us what we otherwise ought ... to do with much greater labour for ourselves" (para. 33).
- **The same decay threatens practices.** "There is only too great a tendency in the best beliefs and practices to degenerate into the mechanical; and unless there were a succession of persons whose ever-recurring originality prevents the grounds of those beliefs and practices from becoming merely traditional, such dead matter would not resist the smallest shock from anything really alive, and there would be no reason why civilisation should not die out, as in the Byzantine Empire" (Ch. 3, para. 11).
