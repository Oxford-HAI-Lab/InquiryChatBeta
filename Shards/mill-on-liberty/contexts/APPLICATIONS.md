# APPLICATIONS: How does the harm principle apply to contested real-world cases?

## Parent context

The harm principle makes prevention of harm to others the sole ground for interference. Ch. 5 tests it against cases. Mill offers "not so much applications, as specimens of application; which may serve to bring into greater clearness the meaning and limits of the two maxims which together form the entire doctrine of this Essay, and to assist the judgment in holding the balance between them, in the cases where it appears doubtful which of them is applicable" (Ch. 5, para. 1). The core finding is that most cases resolve cleanly, but some, above all interested instigation of self-regarding vice, lie "on the exact boundary line between two principles".

## Key claims from source

- **The two maxims.** "First, that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself ... Secondly, that for such actions as are prejudicial to the interests of others, the individual is accountable and may be subjected either to social or to legal punishments" (Ch. 5, para. 2).
- **Harm does not always justify interference.** Competition "necessarily and therefore legitimately causes pain or loss to others"; society interferes "only when means of success have been employed which it is contrary to the general interest to permit—namely, fraud or treachery, and force" (para. 3).
- **Trade.** "Trade is a social act", so in principle within society's jurisdiction; but "the so-called doctrine of Free Trade ... rests on grounds different from, though equally solid with, the principle of individual liberty" (para. 4). Restrictions whose object "is to make it impossible or difficult to obtain a particular commodity" (the Maine Law, the opium prohibition in China, restrictions on poisons) "are objectionable, not as infringements on the liberty of the producer or seller, but on that of the buyer."

## Arguments and evidence

- **Prevention of crime and accident: poisons and the bridge.** "If poisons were never bought or used for any purpose except the commission of murder, it would be right to prohibit their manufacture and sale" (para. 5); since they have useful purposes, prohibition is out. A person about to cross an unsafe bridge may be seized if there is no time to warn him, "for liberty consists in doing what one desires, and he does not desire to fall into the river"; where there is only "a danger of mischief", he "ought ... to be only warned". So "labelling the drug with some word expressive of its dangerous character, may be enforced without violation of liberty", but requiring "the certificate of a medical practitioner, would make it sometimes impossible, always expensive, to obtain the article for legitimate uses." Mill's solution is "preappointed evidence" (Bentham's phrase): "The seller ... might be required to enter into a register the exact time of the transaction, the name and address of the buyer, the precise quality and quantity sold; to ask the purpose for which it was wanted, and record the answer he received."
- **Drunkenness and idleness.** "Drunkenness, for example, in ordinary cases, is not a fit subject for legislative interference; but I should deem it perfectly legitimate that a person, who had once been convicted of any act of violence to others under the influence of drink, should be placed under a special legal restriction, personal to himself" (para. 6). Idleness "cannot without tyranny be made a subject of legal punishment", but a man who thereby fails "to support his children" may be forced to do so "by compulsory labour".
- **Public indecency.** Acts injurious only to the agent, "if done publicly, are a violation of good manners and coming thus within the category of offences against others may rightfully be prohibited" (para. 7).
- **Pimps and gambling-houses: the hard case.** "Whatever it is permitted to do, it must be permitted to advise to do. The question is doubtful, only when the instigator derives a personal benefit from his advice ... Fornication, for example, must be tolerated, and so must gambling; but should a person be free to be a pimp, or to keep a gambling-house? The case is one of those which lie on the exact boundary line between two principles" (para. 8). For toleration: "the fact of following anything as an occupation ... cannot make that criminal which would otherwise be admissible". Against: the State may "exclude the influence of solicitations which are not disinterested, of instigators who cannot possibly be impartial". Mill leaves it open: "I will not venture to decide whether they are sufficient to justify the moral anomaly of punishing the accessary, when the principal is (and must be) allowed to go free". But dealers in drink, "though interested in their abuse, are indispensably required for the sake of their legitimate use", so the Maine Law gains nothing from this argument.
- **Taxing and licensing drink.** "To tax stimulants for the sole purpose of making them more difficult to be obtained, is a measure differing only in degree from their entire prohibition" (para. 9); but since revenue must be raised, the State should prefer "those of which it deems the use, beyond a very moderate quantity, to be positively injurious". Licensing to "persons of known or vouched-for respectability" and regulating hours are legitimate police; limiting the number of houses to reduce temptation treats "the labouring classes ... as children or savages" (para. 10).
- **Contracts and slavery.** Engagements should generally be kept, but one "by which a person should sell himself, or allow himself to be sold, as a slave, would be null and void ... The principle of freedom cannot require that he should be free not to be free" (para. 11). Engagements involving personal relations need liberty of retractation, though obligations to reliant persons and to children "cannot be ignored".
- **Education.** "The State should require and compel the education, up to a certain standard, of every human being who is born its citizen" (para. 12), yet "if the government would make up its mind to *require* for every child a good education, it might save itself the trouble of *providing* one" (para. 13). "A general State education is a mere contrivance for moulding people to be exactly like one another ... it establishes a despotism over the mind, leading by natural tendency to one over the body." State schools "should only exist, if it exist at all, as one among many competing experiments". Enforcement by public examinations "confined to facts and positive science exclusively" (para. 14).
- **Sabbath, prohibition and Mormonism (Ch. 4).** Sabbatarian restrictions on amusements are "illegitimate interference with the rightful liberty of the individual", defensible only on the ground "that they are religiously wrong; a motive of legislation which never can be too earnestly protested against" (Ch. 4, para. 20). The prohibitionist theory of "social rights" would leave "no violation of liberty which it would not justify" (para. 19). Of the Mormons: "No one has a deeper disapprobation than I have of this Mormon institution", yet "it is difficult to see on what principles but those of tyranny they can be prevented from living there under what laws they please, provided they commit no aggression on other nations, and allow perfect freedom of departure to those who are dissatisfied with their ways" (para. 21).

## Distinctions and qualifications

- **Liberty versus power over others.** "A person should be free to do as he likes in his own concerns; but he ought not to be free to do as he likes in acting for another" (Ch. 5, para. 12). Opinion is "more jealous" of interference with a father's power over his children "than of almost any interference with his own freedom of action: so much less do the generality of mankind value liberty than power."
- **Government help, not restraint: three objections.** Questions about the government doing things for people "do not, in strictness, belong" to the Essay (para. 16). The objections are: the thing "is likely to be better done by individuals" (para. 18); doing it themselves is "a means to their own mental education—a mode of strengthening their active faculties" (para. 19); and "the great evil of adding unnecessarily to its power" (para. 20): "If the roads, the railways, the banks, the insurance offices, the great joint-stock companies, the universities, and the public charities, were all of them branches of the government ... not all the freedom of the press and popular constitution of the legislature would make this or any other country free otherwise than in name."
- **The standard.** "The greatest dissemination of power consistent with efficiency; but the greatest possible centralisation of information, and diffusion of it from the centre" (para. 23). "The worth of a State, in the long run, is the worth of the individuals composing it".
